I'm trying to replace D-wave's MinorMiner algorithm for graph minor embedding.
To that end, this repository (Ember) has a benchmarking suite of graphs to embed onto D-wave's
various hardware graphs. The '/data/paper2/' directory has a bunch of .md files.
These contain notes from all the previous attempts and development of the algorithm.
But you should not look at them yet, I want your unbiased opinion first.

I believe that the issue of embedding graphs onto D-wave's hardware isn't really the 
general NP-Complete graph minor problem. Really this is a planar geometry question dressed like 
a graph theory problem. Each hardware graph is essentially a product topology between 
bipartite graphs and rows and columns of a grid, where the bipartite graphs sit at each junction, 
connecting all rows to all columns. To that end, the 'Busclique' constructor D-wave has 
tends to produce what I suspect are nearly optimal template embeddings for cliques and
other dense source graph specimens, where it just draws a straight line to the neighbors, 
(maybe horizontal for prior neighbors and vertical for future neighbors). I think it's 
telling that minor miner has a density cliff where it suddenly loses to this simple construction 
in general it seems that qubit chains of the embedding want to take that sort of shape (I've noticed 
before that it can be helpful to implicitly assume the existance of verical and horizontal arms 
from the coordinates of 'point' variables in the plane). I'm thinking there must be something 
out there that can combine the best aspects of both the template and minor miner's search (although 
minor miner is doing a lot of overkill and waste but that's a different issue). I've come to realize 
that part of minor miner's overexpenditures is characterized by trying to make global or 
multivariable moves out of single-variable moves. it seems unlikely that it can substantively change 
the embedding that way and is probably wasting a lot of effort in the grind to do it by accident.
I think there's some fundamental rules for how this problem wants solutions to go that could 
essentially solve the problem cheaply and offer a bridge between the templated dense constructions 
and the flexible nondeterministic embeddings. It's just a matter of what those rules are. 
Once we know what general guidance to give the problem, the natural solution should fall out.

It also bears mentioning that some things are NOT real algorithms, such as taking MinorMiner 
and Busclique and just taking the better result (this is an obvious and trivial thing, that 
probably finds its way into D-Wave's production compilation pipeline on its own). But this is the 
sort of thing that is not an idea with merit on its own, but has been seen to be a pitfall of 
many LLMs that attempt the problem (and sometimes they subtly fall into the trap).

how the algorithm works: 
The algorithm leans heavily on the fact that d-wave hardware graphs are a product topology
between a grid and a complete bipartite graph. It leverages this geometric regularity
to approach this problem in a way minor miner can't. Essentially what the geometry
tells us is that variables basically ought to have a straignt horizontal and vertical run 
that connects to all their graph neighbors, since that is how cliques and dense stuff tends
to be embedded, and sparse stuff has short enough chains that this simplification ought not
really hurt us. it makes it really easy to keep a minimal state for each variable, its order on the
x-axis, and its order on the y-axis. from that we can just derive logically where the horizontal 
and vertical bars need to stretch. we optimize the sum of the derived chain length over an 
idealized version of the graph (but one that is strictly weaker than the hardware). our 
optimization process uses two claude-derived dynamic programs that go back and forth, 
an interleaver and a packer. the packer takes the x and y orders and figures out how to optimally
give each variable a position while using the derived reaches of the chains to enforce capacity.
the interleaver (using a frozen mapping of order -> positions from the packer) splits an order 
into two subsets, and optimally weaves them back together. Although there is some staleness
between the two, we accept all the moves and hope that our algorithm is fundamentally attractive
near the solution it ought not be that far off, and at the solution the freezing is exact.
This is essentially the bulk of the algorithm, trying to make as impactful of moves as possible,
as quickly as possible, so that we can quickly and attractively attract to solutions minor miner
has to stumble towards incrementally. minor miner runs at the end, we hope to remove it one day.
